Prototyping straight in code

Written by
Daniel Okafor
Design director, product

For years our interface work ended at a beautiful file and began again in someone else's codebase. Somewhere in that gap, spacing changed, motion disappeared and the states nobody drew got invented on the spot. So we moved the gap.

Why the file was never the product

A static frame cannot tell you how a list behaves with forty items, how a heading wraps on a narrow phone, or how long a transition should take before it feels slow. Those are the details people actually notice, and they only exist in a browser.

  • Real content — every screen is built with the client's actual copy and data from week two
  • Real devices — reviews happen on phones and laptops, not on a projected canvas
  • Real motion — timing and easing are decided in code and shipped exactly as approved

What the developers get

When the prototype is the reference, nobody has to interpret what the designer meant.

Developers receive working components rather than pictures of them. They can read the code, question it and adopt what fits their stack. The conversation moves from what something should look like to how it should be built — a far better conversation to have.

Where we still draw first

We still sketch. Early direction work is faster on paper and in a canvas, where a bad idea costs five minutes rather than an afternoon. The rule is simply that nothing gets approved until it has been seen working.

The cost

It asks more of the designers. Everyone on the product team writes enough code to build what they draw, and that took us a year of practice to get right. It was worth every week of it.
