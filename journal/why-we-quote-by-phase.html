Why we quote by phase, not by the hour.

Written by
Yasmin Haddou
Design director

Every studio that charges by the hour is quietly working against itself. The faster you get, the less you earn. The more experience you bring to a problem, the smaller the invoice. Ten years in, we were solving in two days what used to take two weeks, and earning less for it.

What the hour really counts

An hour counts attendance, not results. It tells a client how long someone sat with their problem, which is the one part of the engagement they have no reason to care about. It also puts both sides on opposite ends of the same number: they want fewer hours, we want more, and every conversation about scope turns into a negotiation about time instead of the work.

Worse, it makes the better version of a project the expensive one. A client who asks for one more round of exploration is asking for a larger bill, so they stop asking. The work gets narrower exactly where it should get wider.

How a Norvik phase is priced

We agree a fixed fee per phase before the phase begins. Four numbers, four dates, signed once. What sits inside each fee is written plainly:

  • Discovery — interviews, audit, positioning, and the written brief everything else is judged against
  • Direction — two or three routes, taken far enough to judge, shown in context rather than on a board
  • Build — the chosen route drawn out in full, with the components and tokens the team will really use
  • Launch — production files, handover, and the first weeks in market with us still on the project

If a phase takes us longer than planned, that is our problem, not an invoice. If it takes less, the client keeps the difference as scope, not as change.

The only honest way to charge more is to be worth more to the business, not to be slower at the desk.

What changes in the room

Fixed fees change the conversation on the first day. Nobody counts revisions. Nobody asks whether a call is billable. The question stops being what will this cost and becomes is this the right thing to make — which is the question we are actually good at.

It also forces us to be honest about scope before the work starts. A fixed fee is only safe if the brief is sharp, so the brief gets sharp. Discovery earns its place not because clients enjoy paying for it, but because it is what makes the other three numbers believable.

The harder part

We turn down more work than we used to. A project with no defined shape cannot be priced fixed, and pretending otherwise only moves the risk somewhere it will surface later. When a brief is genuinely unknown, we sell the discovery phase alone and price the rest after — which sometimes means the rest never happens, and that is a fair outcome.
